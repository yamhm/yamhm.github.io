About

My research path runs from molecules to models. I started in bioengineering in high school — designing DNA proximity circuits that detect single-nucleotide mutations without enzymes, and engineering bacteria to clean arsenic from contaminated water. Then I discovered computer science at Stanford, where I’m now a master’s student working under Chris Manning at the Stanford Artificial Intelligence Laboratory.

My research asks how we get AI agents to work in the real world. Real environments offer less than benchmarks promise — less data, messier inputs, tighter compute — so I work on the pieces that close the gap: sample-efficient training, learning from large-scale unstructured data, and speeding up computations that are too slow to be practical, from knowledge distillation for small language models to Monte Carlo–corrected PDE solvers. Along the way I build benchmarks, like WebDS, that measure how far agents still have to go.
