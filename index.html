About

I started out in a wet lab. In high school I spent my afternoons designing DNA circuits that could flag a single mutated nucleotide without any enzymes, and engineering bacteria to pull arsenic out of contaminated water. What hooked me was not the biology itself. It was the idea that you could program matter: write logic into molecules, release them into a messy, unpredictable environment, and watch them work.

At Stanford I found a faster substrate for that obsession. I did my BS in Mathematics, focusing on computer science and theory, and my MS in Computer Science. While there, I worked under Chris Manning in the NLP Group at the Stanford Artificial Intelligence Laboratory, and the question evolved from programming molecules to teaching machines: how much capability can you draw out of a model when data, parameters, and compute are all scarce?

That question now defines my work. I build infrastructure for agents to interact with the world. Real environments offer far less than benchmarks promise: less data, messier inputs, tighter compute. So I work on the pieces that close the gap. I study sample-efficient training and knowledge distillation so small models can punch above their weight, methods for learning from large-scale unstructured data so agents can navigate the web as it actually exists, and ways to accelerate computations too slow to be practical, like Monte Carlo corrected PDE solvers. And I build benchmarks, like WebDS, that measure honestly how far agents still have to go.
